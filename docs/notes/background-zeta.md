# Background: where the zeta function came from

The record behind the site page "Where the zeta function came from". Step 1 speaks of the zeros of the zeta function; this note says what the function is, who made it, and why. Each fact names its source. The page holds the same facts.

## The sum of the inverse squares

- The series 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + … was known to be convergent before Euler. The question of its exact value "can be traced back at least to" Mengoli's treatise of 1650 [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- Wallis, Leibniz, Stirling, de Moivre, Goldbach and several Bernoullis worked on the problem [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- The problem became widely known after Jakob Bernoulli asked it explicitly in 1689. In the same work, Bernoulli considered the series for an arbitrary rational number s [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- Wallis gave a 3-digit approximation in 1655. Euler gave the 7-digit value 1.644934, in a paper presented in 1731; this needed much computing "because the series converges very slowly" [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- In 1735, Euler, 28 years old, found ζ(2) = π²/6, and for each positive whole number ν the value ζ(2ν) = r π^(2ν), with r a rational number [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- Euler wrote: "quite unexpectedly, I have found an elegant formula for ζ(2), depending upon the quadrature of the circle" (translation by Weil) [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- The discovery "made him famous, perhaps for the first time, among mathematicians in all Europe". His teacher Johann Bernoulli exclaimed that "the most burning desire of his deceased older brother Jakob was now fulfilled" [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- Euler tried through his life, without success, to find a formula for ζ(s) for s an odd integer [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- The notation ζ(s) and the name "zeta function" first appear in Riemann's paper [11](../references.md#reference-11), footnote 46.

## The product over the primes

- Euler's paper *Variae observationes circa series infinitas* is of 1737 and was printed in 1744. It "contains the first printed version of Euler's product for Riemann's zeta function" [12](../references.md#reference-12).
- The product is Theorem 8 of that paper: the product of 1/(1 − p^(−s)) over the primes p is equal to the sum of 1/n^s, for s > 1. "Euler only treats the case where s is a positive integer" [12](../references.md#reference-12).
- Theorem 7 is the case s = 1. Euler's steps: from x = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + …, subtract x/2 to remove the even denominators, then a third of the rest to remove the multiples of 3, "and so on until all terms on the right-hand side are deleted". The proof of Theorem 8 "follows the same lines" [12](../references.md#reference-12).
- The page shows these steps for the squares, not for s = 1. The arithmetic of the six parts is an own check. Dividing 1/n² by 4 gives 1/(2n)², so x/4 holds the terms of the even numbers, and (1 − 1/4)x = 1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + 1/49 + 1/81 + …. Dividing 1/n² by 9 gives 1/(3n)², so one ninth of that sum is 1/9 + 1/81 + 1/225 + 1/441 + …, the terms of 3, 9, 15, 21. The difference is (1 − 1/4)(1 − 1/9)x = 1 + 1/25 + 1/49 + 1/121 + …, the terms of 1, 5, 7, 11.
- Since 23 November 2026 the stepper writes each term with its square shown, 1/2² and not 1/4, after the owner's question. The values and the arithmetic are the same; the form shows which whole number each term belongs to and which prime each factor belongs to. Part 5 shows the terms of 1, 5 and 7 only, so that the equation fits a narrow screen; its text names 11 and 13.
- Theorem 19, the last of the paper, is the divergence of the sum of the reciprocals of the primes. As they stand, the statements of Theorems 7 and 19 "are unacceptable"; the strong form is due to Mertens in 1874 [12](../references.md#reference-12).
- The divergence is "in some sense a wide generalization of the fact that the number of primes is infinite", which is Proposition 20 of Book IX of Euclid's *Elements* [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- Euler, in a paper written in 1747 and published in 1751: the order of the primes is "a mystery which human mind will never be able to penetrate" [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- The product formula "was the starting point of Riemann's investigations" [11](../references.md#reference-11).

## Bounded sums, and the primes against the squares

- The page shows by blocks of 1, 2, 4, 8 terms that the sum of 1/n² is at most 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + … = 2, and that each block of the sum of 1/n is at least 1/2. This is an own check: 1/4 + 1/9 ≤ 2/4; the four terms 1/16 to 1/49 are ≤ 4/16; 1/3 + 1/4 ≥ 2/4; the four terms 1/5 to 1/8 are ≥ 4/8. No source is cited for the block method.
- For a general power s, an own check: the block that starts at 2^k has 2^k terms, each at most 1/2^(ks), so the block is at most (2/2^s)^k. For s above 1 the ratio 2/2^s is below 1, so the blocks are bounded by a geometric sum, and the sum of 1/n^s has a value. For s = 1 the ratio is 1. The page states this as "the blocks shrink".
- The page calls the case s = 1 "no power", as the chart does, and says once that this is the power s = 1.
- The divergence of the sum of 1/n has a "14th-century proof" by Nicole Oresme [14](../references.md#reference-14). Whether Oresme used these blocks is not checked; the page only says that he proved the result.
- "Since Σ 1/n = ∞ and Σ 1/p = ∞ while Σ 1/n² < ∞, the primes are 'more dense' among the positive integers than the squares" [13](../references.md#reference-13). The page says "the primes come more often than the squares".
- Reference 13 dates Euler's proof to 1737 and its Theorem 7, and says that Euler knew the rate log(log N); both agree with reference 12.
- The general product on the page, one factor 1/(1 − 1/pˢ) for each prime and s above 1, is Theorem 8 as reference 12 states it. The remark that the term of n divided by 2ˢ is the term of 2n is an own check: 2ˢ × nˢ = (2n)ˢ.

## From Euler to Riemann

- In a paper written in 1749 and published in 1768, Euler found the functional equation, a relation between ζ(n) and ζ(1 − n). He proved it for every positive integer n and conjectured it for all n (Weil's comment) [11](../references.md#reference-11).
- What the symmetry is, asked by the owner on 23 November 2026:
  - Euler does not write the relation between ζ(n) and ζ(1 − n) itself; "it follows from a relation he writes, as pointed out by Weil" [11](../references.md#reference-11).
  - The formula that Euler writes: (1 − 2ⁿ⁻¹ + 3ⁿ⁻¹ − 4ⁿ⁻¹ + …) / (1 − 2⁻ⁿ + 3⁻ⁿ − 4⁻ⁿ + …) = −(n − 1)! (2ⁿ − 1) / ((2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1) πⁿ) × cos(nπ/2) [11](../references.md#reference-11).
  - Weil's comment: the left side is "formally the quotient" of ζ(1 − n) and ζ(n), "except that Euler had written alternating signs to make the series more tractable; the effect of this is merely to multiply ζ(n) by 1 − 2¹⁻ⁿ, and ζ(1 − n) by 1 − 2ⁿ" [11](../references.md#reference-11).
  - The sum on top has no value in the usual sense. Euler proves the formula for every positive integer n "using the so-called Abel summation to give a meaning to the divergent series in the numerator" [11](../references.md#reference-11).
  - Own remark: s and 1 − s have the sum 1, so they are mirror images about 1/2. For example 2 pairs with −1, and 3 pairs with −2.
  - Own check for n = 2. The top is 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + …. For x between 0 and 1, 1 − 2x + 3x² − 4x³ + … = 1/(1 + x)², which goes to 1/4 as x goes to 1. The bottom is 1 − 1/4 + 1/9 − … = (1 − 2⁻¹) × π²/6 = π²/12, by Weil's factor and Euler's value π²/6. The quotient is 3/π². The right side is −1 × 3 / (1 × π²) × cos(π) = 3/π². The two sides agree. That the limit as x goes to 1 is what "Abel summation" means is not looked up.
  - Riemann, 1859: with ξ(s) = π^(−s/2) Γ(s/2) ζ(s), the function ξ extends to the whole plane except for poles at 0 and 1, and ξ(s) = ξ(1 − s) [10](../references.md#reference-10). The same theorem says: "It follows that ζ also extends to a meromorphic function on C, which is regular except for a simple pole at s = 1" [10](../references.md#reference-10). So the symmetry holds for complex s too, away from the two poles of ξ, and ζ has a value at every point except 1.
  - Own remark: at s = −1 the term 1/nˢ is n, so the sum would be 1 + 2 + 3 + …. Its partial sums grow without limit, so it has no value in the usual sense. The page says "has no value" on this basis.
  - Own remark: the formula does not always give one value from the other. For n = 3 the factor cos(3π/2) is 0, so the formula says that the value at −2 is 0 and says nothing about the value at 3. The page gives only the direction from 2 to −1.

- How Euler found the symmetry, asked by the owner on 23 November 2026 (completed through section 20 on 27 November 2026). Read: the full English translation of Euler's paper by Willis and Osler, sections 1 to 20 and all translators' notes, plus visual verification of sections 10 to 13 (pp. 94–97) and sections 15 to 20 (pp. 99–106) on the 1768 French original scan [19](../references.md#reference-19). Section numbers are Euler's.
  - Section 1: the paper is about two series with alternating signs, $1 - 2^m + 3^m - 4^m + \cdots$ and $1 - \frac{1}{2^n} + \frac{1}{3^n} - \frac{1}{4^n} + \cdots$. Euler: "I only demonstrate this relation for certain special cases", but the argument reaches "such a degree of certainty that the reader will conclude it very rigorously shown" [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 2: Euler says that the first series has no sum in the usual sense: after 100 terms $1 - 2 + 3 - \cdots$ is $-50$, after 101 terms $+51$ [19](../references.md#reference-19). "It is necessary to give to the word sum a more extended meaning." The sum $1 - 2 + 3 - 4 + \cdots$ is $\frac{1}{4}$ because the series comes from expanding $\frac{1}{(1 + x)^2}$ at $x = 1$ [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 3: the same method for higher powers gives $\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, 0, -\frac{2}{16}, 0, \frac{16}{64}, 0, \dots$ for $m = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, \dots$ [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 4: for the second series Euler knew $n = 1$ ($\ln 2$). For even $n$ he knew the sums without alternating signs, $1 + \frac{1}{2^n} + \frac{1}{3^n} + \cdots = A\pi^2, B\pi^4, C\pi^6, \dots$ with $A = \frac{1}{6}$, and from them the alternating ones: $\frac{2 - 1}{2}A\pi^2$, $\frac{2^3 - 1}{2^3}B\pi^4$, …. For odd $n$: "all my effort to find their sum is a failure up to now" [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Sections 6 to 8: with his general summation formula he shows that the sums of the first series, for odd $m$, contain the same numbers $A, B, C, \dots$, and are $0$ for even $m$ greater than $0$ [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 9: he divides each series of the first type by the series of the second type with the same number. For $n = 2$ the quotient is $\frac{1 \cdot (2^2 - 1)}{(2 - 1)\pi^2} = \frac{3}{\pi^2}$, which agrees with our own check above [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 10: he writes one formula with a coefficient $N$, lists $N = +1, 0, -1, 0, +1, \dots$ for $n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, \dots$, and takes $N = -\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi n}{2}\right)$. "For this reason I venture to propose the following conjecture, that for any variable n, the following equation is always valid". "This conjecture will undoubtedly stand out as bold" [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Sections 11 and 12: he checks $n = 1$ and $n = 0$ by a limit. For $n = 1$ both sides are $\frac{1}{2\ln 2}$; for $n = 0$ both sides are $2\ln 2$. After section 12: "we have not given a direct demonstration that contains at once all the possible cases" [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 13: with $[\lambda]\,[-\lambda] = \frac{\lambda\pi}{\sin(\lambda\pi)}$ for his factorial notation, he shows that the formula for a negative integer follows from the formula for a positive integer. Checked also on pp. 96–97 of the 1768 French original [19](../references.md#reference-19), where Euler writes *"une observation, que j'ai prouvée ailleurs"* without naming the earlier work (for our direct check of E122 [40](../references.md#reference-40) and E101 [41](../references.md#reference-41) before 1749, see [background-gamma.md](background-gamma.md)).
  - Section 14: for $n = \frac{1}{2}$ the two series are the same, so the quotient is $1$, and the formula gives $1$ as well. The translators note that this is the only case in the paper where both series converge [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 15: for $n = \frac{3}{2}$ he computes both series numerically and finds $\frac{0.380129}{0.765158}$ against the formula's $0.4967738$ (1768 original pp. 99–100) [19](../references.md#reference-19). The translators note arithmetic slips in Euler's digits; with correct digits the agreement is closer [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 16 (pp. 100–101 of the 1768 French original [19](../references.md#reference-19)): treating the conjecture as established for fractional exponents as well, Euler displays seven alternating-series quotients for real half-integer exponents $n = \frac{2i + 1}{2}$ with positive integer index $i = 1, 2, \dots, 7$ (so $n = \frac{3}{2}, \frac{5}{2}, \dots, \frac{15}{2}$, with signs $+, +, -, -, +, +, -$; the 1768 print on p. 100 has a minor typo $4\sqrt{3}$ for $4\sqrt{4}$ in the first denominator). Writing $\lambda$ for the same positive integer index ($n = \lambda + \frac{1}{2}$), he simplifies the prefactor $\frac{2^\lambda\sqrt{2} - 1}{2^\lambda - \sqrt{2}} = \frac{(2^{2\lambda} - 1)\sqrt{2} + 2^\lambda}{2^{2\lambda} - 2}$ and notes that for each pair of half-integer series, knowing one sum gives the other *"par le moyen de la quadrature du cercle"* (through $\pi$) [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Sections 17 and 18 (pp. 101–103 of the 1768 French original [19](../references.md#reference-19)): Euler returns to his longstanding goal of summing the alternating reciprocal odd-power series $1 - \frac{1}{2^{2\lambda + 1}} + \frac{1}{3^{2\lambda + 1}} - \frac{1}{4^{2\lambda + 1}} + \cdots$ for positive integer $\lambda$. Rewriting his conjecture for $n = 2\lambda + 1$ puts both the numerator $1 - 2^{2\lambda} + 3^{2\lambda} - 4^{2\lambda} + \cdots$ and the denominator $\cos\!\left(\frac{2\lambda + 1}{2}\pi\right) = -\sin(\lambda\pi)$ equal to $0$ at every integer $\lambda \ge 1$ [19](../references.md#reference-19). Applying L'Hôpital's rule (differentiating numerator and denominator with respect to $\lambda$) yields
    $$1 - \frac{1}{2^{2\lambda + 1}} + \frac{1}{3^{2\lambda + 1}} - \frac{1}{4^{2\lambda + 1}} + \cdots = \frac{2(2^{2\lambda} - 1)\pi^{2\lambda}}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots 2\lambda\,(2^{2\lambda + 1} - 1)\cos(\lambda\pi)}\left(1^{2\lambda}\ln 1 - 2^{2\lambda}\ln 2 + 3^{2\lambda}\ln 3 - 4^{2\lambda}\ln 4 + \cdots\right),$$
    which Euler writes out explicitly for $n = 3, 5, 7, 9, 11$ on p. 103 (on p. 102 the general formula misprints $4^{2\lambda}\ln 2$ for $4^{2\lambda}\ln 4$, corrected in all five displayed cases on p. 103) [19](../references.md#reference-19). Euler concludes that evaluating $1^{2\lambda}\ln 1 - 2^{2\lambda}\ln 2 + 3^{2\lambda}\ln 3 - 4^{2\lambda}\ln 4 + \cdots$ *"est peut-être plus difficile que celle que nous avons en vue; & je n'entrevois aucune méthode qui nous puisse conduire au but proposé"* [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 19 (pp. 103–104 of the 1768 French original [19](../references.md#reference-19)): Euler drops the $\frac{2^{2\lambda} - 1}{2^{2\lambda + 1} - 1}$ factor by passing to the convergent non-alternating odd-denominator series $1 + \frac{1}{3^m} + \frac{1}{5^m} + \frac{1}{7^m} + \cdots = \frac{2^m - 1}{2(2^{m - 1} - 1)}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2^m} + \frac{1}{3^m} - \frac{1}{4^m} + \cdots\right)$, obtaining for positive integers $\lambda = 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$
    $$1 + \frac{1}{3^{2\lambda + 1}} + \frac{1}{5^{2\lambda + 1}} + \frac{1}{7^{2\lambda + 1}} + \cdots = -\frac{\pi^{2\lambda}}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots 2\lambda\,\cos(\lambda\pi)}\left(2^{2\lambda}\ln 2 - 3^{2\lambda}\ln 3 + 4^{2\lambda}\ln 4 - 5^{2\lambda}\ln 5 + \cdots\right),$$
    displayed for $\lambda = 1, 2, 3, 4$ on p. 104 (with a printer's misprint $\frac{1}{3^4}$ for $\frac{1}{3^5}$ in the second line) [19](../references.md#reference-19). He cautions that this fails at $\lambda = 0$ because $1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots = \frac{1}{2} \neq 0$, yet notes that $\ln 2 - \ln 3 + \ln 4 - \ln 5 + \cdots = \frac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ (the logarithmic form of Wallis's product), giving *"quelque espérance"* of eventually summing these logarithmic series [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Section 20 (pp. 105–106 of the 1768 French original [19](../references.md#reference-19)): in the same way, Euler compares the alternating odd-integer series $1 - 3^{n - 1} + 5^{n - 1} - 7^{n - 1} + \cdots$ and $1 - \frac{1}{3^n} + \frac{1}{5^n} - \frac{1}{7^n} + \cdots$ (in modern terms, the Dirichlet beta function $\beta(s) = L(s, \chi_4)$) and states the parallel conjecture
    $$\frac{1 - 3^{n - 1} + 5^{n - 1} - 7^{n - 1} + \cdots}{1 - 3^{-n} + 5^{-n} - 7^{-n} + \cdots} = \frac{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots (n - 1)\,2^n}{\pi^n}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi}{2}\right).$$
    At positive even integers $n = 2\lambda$, both numerator and $\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)$ vanish; differentiating gives $1 - \frac{1}{3^{2\lambda}} + \frac{1}{5^{2\lambda}} - \frac{1}{7^{2\lambda}} + \cdots = -\frac{\pi^{2\lambda - 1}}{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots (2\lambda - 1)\,2^{2\lambda - 1}\cos(\lambda\pi)}\left(3^{2\lambda - 1}\ln 3 - 5^{2\lambda - 1}\ln 5 + 7^{2\lambda - 1}\ln 7 - \cdots\right)$, written out for $\lambda = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ (the 1768 original p. 105 prints the correct denominator $1 \cdot 2^1$ for $\lambda = 1$, whereas Willis and Osler misprint $1 \cdot 2^2$; their translator's note to section 20 also errs in writing $L(s) = \frac{\zeta(s) + \eta(s)}{2}$, which equals $1 + \frac{1}{3^s} + \frac{1}{5^s} + \cdots$ rather than $1 - \frac{1}{3^s} + \frac{1}{5^s} - \cdots$) [19](../references.md#reference-19). Euler closes by hoping this simpler conjecture will find *"une démonstration parfaite"* [19](../references.md#reference-19).
  - Non-alternating values in E352 (checked 27 November 2026 across all 20 sections [19](../references.md#reference-19)): Euler **never** writes down the divergent non-alternating values $\zeta(-1) = -\frac{1}{12}$ ($1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots$), $\zeta(0) = -\frac{1}{2}$ ($1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots$), or $\zeta(-2) = 0$ ($1 + 2^2 + 3^2 + \cdots$) anywhere in E352. Non-alternating series appear in E352 only as convergent reciprocal sums for $s > 1$: $1 + \frac{1}{2^{2k}} + \frac{1}{3^{2k}} + \cdots$ in section 4 (used to compute the alternating sums) and $1 + \frac{1}{3^{2\lambda + 1}} + \frac{1}{5^{2\lambda + 1}} + \cdots$ in section 19 (used to simplify the prefactor) [19](../references.md#reference-19). Every divergent direct-power series ($m \ge 0$) and every functional-equation conjecture in E352 stays strictly alternating, where Euler's generating-function and Euler–Maclaurin method (sections 2–3 and 7) eliminates the integral $\int X\,dx$ and assigns finite values ($\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{4}, 0, -\frac{1}{8}, 0, \dots$) [19](../references.md#reference-19).

- Did Euler (or anyone before Riemann in 1859) evaluate $\zeta(s)$ or the alternating zeta series at complex values of $s$? (checked 27 November 2026):
  - **Primary Euler works checked directly:** In E352 (all 20 sections read in full) [19](../references.md#reference-19), Euler never considers complex or imaginary values of the exponent $n$: he tests only positive integers (sections 1–11), $n = 0$ (section 12), negative integers (section 13), and positive real half-integers ($n = \frac{1}{2}$ in section 14, $n = \frac{3}{2}$ in section 15, and $n = \frac{2k + 1}{2}$ for positive integer $k$ — Euler's integer index $i$ — in section 16). Likewise, in E72 (*Variae observationes circa series infinitas*, 1737/1744, English translation) [49](../references.md#reference-49), Euler states the Euler product (Theorem 8) for positive integer exponents $n$ and contains zero mentions of imaginary or complex exponents across all 18 pages.
  - **Scholarly consensus:** Although Euler included complex values for general functions in his *Introductio* [11](../references.md#reference-11) (section 3), every directly read scholarly survey — Papadopoulos (citing Weil) [11](../references.md#reference-11) (pp. 34–39), Lagarias [50](../references.md#reference-50) (pp. 18–20, 31), and Bombieri [51](../references.md#reference-51) (pp. 1–3) — agrees that for the zeta series Euler worked over real and integer arguments, and that Bernhard Riemann in November 1859 was the first to treat $\zeta(s)$ as a function of a complex variable $s$. Riemann's memoir itself [43](../references.md#reference-43) (PDF p. 2) opens directly from Euler's product–sum identity and declares: *"The function of the complex variable $s$ which is represented by these two expressions, wherever they converge, I denote by $\zeta(s)$.   - **Precursors between Euler and Riemann (real-variable methods):**
    - **Dirichlet:** in 1837 Dirichlet proved the infinitude of primes in arithmetic progressions using Dirichlet $L$-functions and already used the variable letter $s$ (page 1 footnote 1 and page 2) [51](../references.md#reference-51); Dirichlet's memoir itself was not read directly.
    - **Carl Johan Malmsten (1849, dated 1 May 1846):** In *De integralibus quibusdam definitis, seriebusque infinitis* (*Crelle's Journal* 38, pp. 1–39) [52](../references.md#reference-52), Malmsten rigorously proved functional equations for Dirichlet $L$-series, including $L(s, \chi_4) = 1 - \frac{1}{3^s} + \frac{1}{5^s} - \cdots$ (eq. (51), p. 17, matching Euler's E352 section 20 conjecture) and $L(s, \chi_3) = 1 - \frac{1}{2^s} + \frac{1}{4^s} - \frac{1}{5^s} + \cdots$ (eq. (52), p. 18), remarking in Latin on p. 18 that he remembered seeing (51) and (52) found by Euler by induction without proof [52](../references.md#reference-52). Visually checked on pp. 12 and 16 of the GDZ scan [52](../references.md#reference-52): Malmsten explicitly restricts $s$ to the real open interval $1 > s > 0$ (p. 12, under eqs. (27) and (29)) and applies the real mean-value bound $1 > \Theta > 0$ (p. 16). As Aycock notes [54](../references.md#reference-54) (pp. 1–2, 4), Malmsten did not state the functional equation of $\zeta(s)$ or $\eta(s)$ itself and did not introduce complex analytic continuation.
    - **Pafnuty Chebyshev (presented 1848, reprinted 1852):** In *Sur la totalité des nombres premiers inférieurs à une limite donnée* (*JMPA* 17, pp. 341–365) [55](../references.md#reference-55) (summarized also by Bombieri [51](../references.md#reference-51), p. 2), Chebyshev analyzed $\sum_{m = 2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{m^{1 + \rho}} = \zeta(1 + \rho) - 1$ and the logarithm of the Euler product to study $\pi(x)$ analytically. ## Why the negative even numbers are "trivial" zeros

Added on 28 November 2026 after the owner asked why $-2, -4, -6, \dots$ are trivial zeros, and how and when they were proven.

- **Two equivalent derivations of $\zeta(-2n) = 0$ for $n \ge 1$:**
  - *From the completed symmetry:* $\zeta(s) = \frac{\Lambda(s)}{\pi^{-\frac{s}{2}}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)}$ where $\Lambda(s) = \Lambda(1 - s)$ is finite everywhere except $s = 0$ and $s = 1$ [10](../references.md#reference-10) [43](../references.md#reference-43) (PDF pp. 3–4). At $s = -2n$ ($n \ge 1$), $\Lambda(-2n) = \Lambda(1 + 2n)$ is finite and nonzero, whereas $\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s}{2}\right) = \Gamma(-n)$ has a pole, forcing $\zeta(-2n) = 0$ (own check; also stated in Landau [61](../references.md#reference-61), Vol. 1 § 70, p. 284). At $s = 0$, the numerator $\Lambda(s)$ also has a pole, cancelling the pole of $\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)$ to give $\zeta(0) = -\frac{1}{2}$ (Mathlib `riemannZeta_zero` and `differentiableAt_hurwitzZetaEven` at commit `56a00585` [37](../references.md#reference-37)).
  - *From the direct symmetry equation:* `riemannZeta_one_sub` gives $\zeta(1 - s) = 2\,(2\pi)^{-s}\,\Gamma(s)\,\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\,\zeta(s)$ for $s \notin \{1, 0, -1, -2, \dots\}$ (Mathlib `RiemannZeta.lean:178-180`, commit `56a00585` [37](../references.md#reference-37)). At odd integers $s = 3, 5, 7, \dots$, every other factor on the right is finite while $\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) = 0$, giving $\zeta(-2) = \zeta(-4) = \zeta(-6) = \cdots = 0$ directly (own check; $s = 1$ is excluded because $\zeta(1)$ has a pole).
  - *In Lean / Mathlib:* `Mathlib/NumberTheory/LSeries/RiemannZeta.lean:173-174` proves `riemannZeta_neg_two_mul_nat_add_one (n : ℕ) : riemannZeta (-2 * (n + 1)) = 0` via `hurwitzZetaEven_neg_two_mul_nat_add_one` (`HurwitzZetaEven.lean:605-610`, commit `56a00585` [37](../references.md#reference-37)), using `completedRiemannZeta s / Gammaℝ s` with `Gammaℝ (-2 * (n + 1)) = 0`, while `differentiableAt_hurwitzZetaEven` (lines 615–621) confirms continuity and differentiability there so $0$ is the true analytic value.
- **When and how they were proven:**
  - *1749 precursor (Euler E352, alternating series only):* Euler evaluated the alternating series $1 - 2^m + 3^m - 4^m + \cdots$ as $0$ for positive even $m$ and wrote $-\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi n}{2}\right)$ in his conjectured ratio formula (§§ 3, 6–10) [19](../references.md#reference-19), without rigorously continuing $\zeta(s)$ or stating $\zeta(-2n) = 0$.
  - *November 1859 first proof (Bernhard Riemann):* In the 1859 *Monatsberichte* original (p. 672; 1876 *Werke*, p. 137) [59](../references.md#reference-59) and Wilkins's translation (PDF p. 3 / translated p. 2) [43](../references.md#reference-43), immediately after deriving the contour-integral representation $2\sin(\pi s)\,\Pi(s - 1)\,\zeta(s) = i\int_\infty^\infty \frac{(-x)^{s - 1}\,dx}{e^x - 1}$, Riemann writes: *"Diese Gleichung giebt nun den Werth der Function $\zeta(s)$ für jedes beliebige complexe $s$ und zeigt, daſs sie einwerthig und für alle endlichen Werthe von $s$, auſser $1$, endlich ist, so wie auch, daſs sie verschwindet, wenn $s$ gleich einer negativen geraden Zahl ist."*
- **Origin of the term "trivial zeros" / *"triviale Nullstellen"* (checked 28 November 2026):**
  - Riemann never uses *trivial*, *triviale Nullstellen*, or *Nullstelle* anywhere in his 1859 paper; on pp. 674–675 he calls the zeros of $\xi(t)$ *"Wurzeln von $\xi(t) = 0$"* / *"reelle Wurzeln"* [59](../references.md#reference-59).
  - Jeff Miller's *Earliest Known Uses of Some of the Words of Mathematics* has no entry for *"trivial zero(s)"* [63](../references.md#reference-63), and directly searched 1893–1905 primary papers by Hadamard (1893, 1896), von Mangoldt (1895, 1905), de la Vallée Poussin (1896), and Landau (1903) contain no occurrences of *trivial* for zeta zeros.
  - The earliest directly verified primary occurrences found here are by **Edmund Landau** — first in *Math. Ann.* 66 (1909, signed Berlin, 21 June 1908), § 4, pp. 437 and 440 (*"nicht trivialen Wurzeln von $L(s)$"*, *"die trivialen Nullstellen"*) [60](../references.md#reference-60), and then in his *Handbuch der Lehre von der Verteilung der Primzahlen* (1909, Vorwort dated 13 September 1909), Vol. 1, §§ 128 and 130, pp. 497–498 and 507–508 (*"die trivialen Wurzeln erster Ordnung"*, *"Alle nicht trivialen ... Nullstellen"*, separating *"die trivialen Wurzeln $< 0$"* from *"den mysteriösen Wurzeln im Streifen $0 < \sigma < 1$"*) and Vol. 2, § 239, p. 866 [61](../references.md#reference-61).
  - In both 1908/1909 works Landau applies *"trivial"* / *"nicht trivial"* specifically in the chapters on Dirichlet $L$-functions $L(s, \chi)$, whereas for $\zeta(s)$ itself he still contrasts the zeros at $-2q$ with *"nicht reelle"* / *"komplexe Nullstellen"* [60](../references.md#reference-60) (p. 419), [61](../references.md#reference-61) (Vol. 1, pp. 284–285, 313–316, 337); the same real-vs.-complex distinction persists in Landau (1912), Bohr & Landau (1913), and E. C. Titchmarsh's 1930 Cambridge Tract *The Zeta-Function of Riemann* (pp. 2–3, *"other than the real ones already observed. These zeros must be complex"*, with zero occurrences of *"trivial zeros"*) [62](../references.md#reference-62).

## Why the zeros control the primes

Added on 28 November 2026 to bridge the Step 1 question of why the zeros of $\zeta(s)$ (rather than other values) govern the distribution of primes.

- **From Euler's product to Riemann's explicit formula (1859):** Starting from $\log\zeta(s) = -\sum_p \log(1 - p^{-s}) = \sum_p p^{-s} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_p p^{-2s} + \frac{1}{3}\sum_p p^{-3s} + \cdots$, Riemann converts this into $\frac{\log\zeta(s)}{s} = \int_1^\infty f(x) x^{-s - 1}\,dx$ for his weighted prime-power count $f(x) = F(x) + \frac{1}{2} F\!\left(x^{\frac{1}{2}}\right) + \frac{1}{3} F\!\left(x^{\frac{1}{3}}\right) + \cdots$ (where $F(x)$ counts primes below $x$), applies Fourier inversion, and substitutes the product of $\xi(t)$ over its roots $\alpha$ (`[43]`, PDF pp. 5–9) [43](../references.md#reference-43).
- **Each zero adds a wave to the prime count:** This yields Riemann's explicit formula (`[43]`, PDF p. 9) [43](../references.md#reference-43):
  $$f(x) = \operatorname{Li}(x) - \sum_\alpha \left(\operatorname{Li}\!\left(x^{\frac{1}{2} + \alpha i}\right) + \operatorname{Li}\!\left(x^{\frac{1}{2} - \alpha i}\right)\right) + \int_x^\infty \frac{dx}{x(x^2 - 1)\log x} + \log\xi(0),$$
  and differentiating a finite truncation yields his prime-density wave sum $\frac{1}{\log x} - 2\sum_\alpha \frac{\cos(\alpha\log x)\,x^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{\log x}$ (`[43]`, PDF p. 9) [43](../references.md#reference-43). On PDF p. 10, Riemann notes that $\operatorname{Li}(x)$ approximates $F(x)$ *"up to quantities of the order $x^{\frac{1}{2}}$"* and highlights *"the increase and decrease in the density of the primes from place to place that is dependent on the periodic terms"* [43](../references.md#reference-43).
- **Why $\operatorname{Re}(\rho)$ sets the wave size (own check of $x^\rho$):** For any complex zero $\rho = \beta + i\gamma$, $x^\rho = x^\beta\,x^{i\gamma} = x^\beta\bigl(\cos(\gamma\log x) + i\sin(\gamma\log x)\bigr)$ has modulus $x^\beta$ and oscillates with frequency $\gamma$ in $\log x$. Because mirrored zeros come in pairs $\beta$ and $1 - \beta$, placing all non-trivial zeros on $\beta = \frac{1}{2}$ minimizes the maximum real part (keeping every wave at size $\sqrt{x}$ rather than a larger power $x^{1 - \beta}$).
- **Modern explicit payoff (checked in Lean):** Lowell Schoenfeld (1976, *Math. Comp.* 30, Theorem 10 (6.2)) showed the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the explicit bound $\left|\psi(x) - x\right| < \frac{\sqrt{x}\log^2 x}{8\pi}$ for all $x \ge 73.2$ (where Chebyshev's $\psi(x)$ is the weighted prime-power sum, equal to $\log(\operatorname{lcm}(1, 2, \dots, n))$ for whole numbers $n$, confirmed in Mathlib `Mathlib/NumberTheory/Chebyshev.lean` at `56a00585`), and Andrew Sutherland has verified both the equivalence and the bound itself end-to-end from `lsernau/grh-lean@72fead10` (`grh-compact/`) with `lake comparator --paranoid` [38](../references.md#reference-38). Own check of the intuition and scale: every time $n$ hits a prime $p$ or a prime power $p^k$, $\operatorname{lcm}(1, 2, \dots, n)$ multiplies by $p$, so $\log(\operatorname{lcm}(1, 2, \dots, n))$ jumps by $\log p$, cancelling the $\frac{1}{\log x}$ thinning of the primes so $\psi(x)$ tracks $x$; "explicit" means concrete constants ($\frac{1}{8\pi}$ and $x \ge 73.2$) rather than an unspecified asymptotic constant; and at $x = 1{,}000{,}000$, $\frac{\sqrt{x}\log^2 x}{8\pi} \approx 7{,}594.41$ (under $0.8\%$ of $x$).

- Own explanation, no source: the product over the primes up to N is the sum of 1/n^s over every n that has only these primes as factors. So the product is never below the sum of the first N terms. At N = 1 both are 1; from N = 2 on, the product is above the sum.
